Notes from Dr. Shailesh Kumar’s keynote, “Intelligent operating system as a service: the third wave in digital transformation”, at Wayfair AI Nexus, Bengaluru, 9 Oct 2026. Shailesh is Chief Data Scientist at Jio and Dean of Jio Institute. This is my paraphrase from a recording, not a transcript. Any mistakes are mine. Draft for friends, not yet reviewed by him.

Automation, intelligence, integration#

Shailesh opened with three waves of digital transformation. The first was automation. The second, where most of us are today, is intelligence. The third is integration. When he thinks of integration, the word that comes to mind is operating system: platforms that everyone builds on, offered as a service. In his words, SaaS is going to die and OS-as-a-service is coming [check: garbled in the recording].

Electricity was the grid, not the bulb#

His first analogy was electricity. It became a platform, and everything we use plugs into it. But we didn’t get electricity by solving each of its use cases one at a time. Bulbs, solar panels, transformers, switches and wires were all needed. The secret was the grid, which connects production, distribution and consumption. The grid mattered more than any single use case.

The Buddha approach#

He then used the Buddha as a model of an innovator, and drew three qualities from his story.

  1. Find the right problem. The Buddha was about to become a king. He went out into the city, saw a sick man, an old man and a dead man, and decided the problem worth solving was human suffering, not running a kingdom. In our world, the question is the same: is the right problem search, LLMs, supply chain? What is the right problem in your domain?
  2. Decide first, learn second. He did not know how to solve it when he decided to. Learning comes after you choose the problem.
  3. Solve it for everyone, forever. Mother Teresa addressed suffering one person at a time. The Buddha tried to solve it for all of mankind, forever. One is a services approach, the other a platform approach. Shailesh’s example: IT services firms like Infosys, Wipro and TCS solve one use case for one bank, then another use case for another bank. A platform company solves a problem for everyone. [His example of a “Buddha company” is unclear in the recording.]

Reimagine#

He said there are two kinds of people in any room. Some go deep into one technology and improve it forever: search, analytics, 3D printers. Others reimagine what the world could be with all these technologies. Those are the people who built the platforms we use: Wayfair, Ola, Uber, Swiggy.

To reimagine is to ask: if we were starting today, how would we do this differently? How would education look if we designed it now? Our current education system looks archaic next to what is possible.

His example was buying a chair. Today you search, you browse, you try things out. Instead, imagine telling a design tool your height, your weight and your back problems [check], and it designs a chair just for you. That chair doesn’t exist in any supply chain, so you press print and a 3D printer makes it. Search gets replaced by design, and the supply chain by printing. He said most of the retail business can be reimagined this way.

He read out a prediction: wireless would connect the whole earth into one brain, people would see each other face to face across distances, and the instruments would be very small. It was said in 1926. [Speaker unclear in the recording; check.] That is the skill he wants to build: imagining a world that does not exist yet.

Why companies die or survive#

Two case studies.

Netflix. People rented movies from stores, forgot to return them, and paid late fines. Netflix asked why you should go to a store at all. It mailed movies to your home and let you keep them as long as you wanted. It understood the customer’s pain, changed the business process, and became one of the first digital-physical companies. Then it did one more thing: it became a data company. It collected data on who watched what and built one of the first recommendation engines. A change in the business process led to a whole new kind of company.

Kodak. Kodak invented the digital camera, then held back because its film business was doing well. If it had pioneered digital photography, it could have owned what later became photo sharing and video platforms. If you won’t disrupt your own business, someone else will. Somebody is already thinking about printing chairs.

From point solutions to a health operating system#

Healthcare today is a set of point solutions. One app tells you what might be wrong, another finds a doctor, another books a lab test at home, another delivers medicine. We are all solving the bulbs, the switches and the wires, and nobody is building the electricity. What we need is a health operating system. As a patient, he doesn’t want four apps.

He pointed at apps like MyJio, Paytm and Bajaj Finance. They show all their offerings in a single UI, with no connection in the background. They were supposed to build a super-app.

The last four digital platforms#

He then laid out what he called the last four digital platforms, and invited the room to challenge him over lunch.

  1. Domain operating system. Walmart, Flipkart and Amazon run the same five or six workflows. So why not write a retail operating system once, and let each company customize it? Today every bank has its own IT team and AI team building its own version. That is like every company generating its own electricity 200 years ago.
  2. Life super-app. Your phone has hundreds of apps. A super-app integrates your life into one place. It knows your real context: not a large token window, but what is going on in your life. Ola knows you only from your rides, Zomato only from your food. A super-app that does everything knows you holistically [check]. It can also push: it tells you it’s time to go home, which he called a very important shift. You agree, and it looks at your data and your options, picks a ride and books it, without you opening an app. He sees each of us ending up with one life super-app and one work super-app. Others are already trying: Musk bought Twitter to build X as an everything app, and Alexa+ and Tata Neu are heading the same way [check].
  3. Work super-app. One per role. Nobody has built a product for what a lawyer or a teacher does all day. Salesforce is a work super-app for salespeople. The Uber driver app is one for drivers. Everyone else uses generic tools: Excel, Gmail, WhatsApp, calls.
  4. AI marketplace. AI is a horizontal everyone needs, so not everyone should build it. That would be like everyone growing their own food.

He called this a trillion-dollar idea: whoever builds all four has built the last four digital platforms.

When to use an agent#

He described the loop a super-app runs when you say “I’m hungry”: a trigger (pull or push), enrichment from your profile, exploration of the options, optimization based on where you are and what you like, execution in the digital and physical world, and learning from how it went.

Then he asked when you should actually use an agent. His answer: the real skill of an agent is when you don’t know the workflow and want to discover one. If you know the workflow, or you want a deterministic answer, you don’t need an agent. Amazon delivering a packet to a location needs a deterministic answer. About 90% of industry use cases are deterministic and well defined. He called this his current understanding.

YouTube and BookMyShow never talk#

About eight years ago he was following an artist on YouTube, so YouTube knew he liked that artist. BookMyShow knew the artist was performing in his city. Both apps were on his phone. They never talked to each other, and he missed the show. The third wave is not about YouTube or BookMyShow being intelligent. It is about connecting them.

Two more examples. You ask your super-app for your favourite pizza. More useful than delivering it is checking your health data first and saying your cholesterol is high. And when two people plan a movie, their agents could compare preferences, location and calendars, find the right show, and book tickets and rides. That is integration between people and across applications. Whoever builds the super-app as an operating system first wins the race. Amazon can stay in retail and Swiggy in food, but whoever builds the whole thing wins.

The AI marketplace#

He returned to the marketplace. Every sector has many beneficiaries using many applications, and each needs hundreds of AI services. Hiring 10,000 data scientists to build all of them is like buying cars to solve transportation. The Buddha approach is to connect producers of AI with consumers of AI, the way Ola, Uber, YouTube and Amazon connect producers and consumers.

His example was the Machli app for fishermen, from a Reliance Foundation video [check who built it]. The fishermen need text-to-speech in Indian coastal languages. Now imagine a PhD from IIIT Hyderabad who has built a good Telugu text-to-speech model uploads it to a marketplace. The two never meet, but the app can now speak Telugu. He described it as an easier version of Hugging Face [check].

The domain operating system#

He described three generations of products. The first was programmed. The second, like Google Search and Facebook, learned from planet-scale feedback: what you clicked, what you searched. The next generation is complex systems: a smart city, a country’s agriculture or healthcare, an e-commerce ecosystem.

Most old companies built these by buying many vendor products. Search from one vendor, supply chain from another, twenty components that don’t talk to each other, not even on the same customer ID. It is like putting a Ferrari on a dirt road. Many are now skipping the foundations and trying to build agents on top.

To simplify a complex system, he breaks it into systems and workflows. An airline has systems to book a flight, check in, handle baggage, and serve food. Every domain has:

  • systems of record: the raw and aggregated data;
  • systems of intelligence: not one model, but hundreds of models running the business;
  • systems of interaction: the website, the app, the terminal;
  • and the missing one, a system of integration.

If search shows a top result that isn’t in inventory, search and inventory can’t be built independently. Something has to ask: can I deliver what I am showing? He compared the system of integration to the body’s nervous system. Without it, we would be a collection of organs that don’t talk to each other.

A retail example#

In retail, you collect raw data from search, customer care, returns, ratings, reviews and recommendations. You build a 360 view of the customer, location, product and brand, down to deep customer profiles as embeddings. Then two customers searching for “men’s shirt” or “sofa” see different results.

He described three parallel data-science streams building customer profiles independently, for separate businesses [check: which businesses]. His question was whether they could be built jointly, and extended with the media business: music, movies, TV. Someone who listens to bhajans in the morning might want a kurta. Integration beyond one vertical keeps creating new insight.

Five universal workflows#

Every domain runs the same few workflows:

  • execution: how do you deliver a product?
  • root cause: something is failing; why?
  • expansion: where should the next store, cell tower or flight go?
  • efficiency: what does efficiency mean for Ola, or for a movie theatre?
  • explanation: how do you explain a decision to the end user?

The goal is not to become an AI person but to become a workflow person, which you can’t do without knowing the domain. Workflow thinking comes from the domain. Systems thinking comes from AI and IT. Unless these two teams work together, you don’t get a complete operating system.

The power of integration#

Cameras and crime. Cities have cameras, yet crimes still happen, and police pull memory sticks out of cameras after the fact. We have built cameras, face detection and action detection. What is missing is integration. The camera company only does cameras, the AI company only does AI.

Advice for farmers. The platform problem in agriculture is daily advice to every farmer. A data scientist starts by asking what AI to use. The process starts elsewhere: understand the farmer’s speech, build a knowledge graph of agriculture (like a product catalogue), build a farmer profile (like a customer profile), integrate real-time sensors, then give advice. AI is not the starting point. The workflow is. The hotel is the goal, not the bulb in the hotel.

Doctors. A mother in a village has a five-year-old with a vomiting episode, and there is no doctor nearby. Imagine an AI diagnosis system that talks with her, then a real doctor confirms the diagnosis. The AI suggests tests, the lab is integrated, the AI reads the report, and the doctor confirms the prescription. Humans become curators of what AI does. Doctors will, and teachers will too. Productivity goes up. But he named the risk: if people become dumber, that is a failure. The real challenge is to preserve human creativity and intelligence while AI gets more intelligent.

Work backward from the operating system#

Picture the retail operating system as a circle of workflows: orders coming in from anywhere, demand forecasting, delivery from the right warehouse. Work backward from the whole system and ask what is missing and whether everything works. Then improve.

A retail OS runs on events from online and offline channels, digital twins of customers, suppliers and stores, a tagged knowledge base of products, and all the AI models running the five or six workflows. Once one retailer has solved twenty problems, it can sell that as a platform to other retailers. That is a retailer’s AWS moment. He thinks in operating systems because he saw the same pattern across five or six businesses in a conglomerate: finance, telecom, retail. He said “we are building” one [check: who, and at what stage].

When everything is connected, intelligence becomes emergent. You order a product and the inventory updates on the other side, because everything is connected. If we had only perfected the car’s engine for 200 years and never launched, we would have no car.

Integrate first, then improve#

His close: integrate first, then improve. The amoeba was the first integrated life form, and everything that came later built on it. Integrate first, then improve, is a better way of working than improving first and stopping there.

From the Q&A#

Where does differentiation come from, if everyone builds the same platforms? We all have the same body. The difference is how we live: diet, exercise, smoking. Likewise, the workflows are common. Differentiation comes from your data, your customer insights and lower-level improvements. A supplier doesn’t know who buys its toothpaste. The retailer in the middle does. A search pipeline of query processing, enrichment and personalization isn’t unique. The data that powers it is.

On AI-written code. He asked the room what the most important metric of code is. Is it how quickly you build it? His answer was maintainability. In five years, nobody will understand what is going on in AI-written code, and you won’t know where in the codebase to make a small change. His first advice: make sure some people in your company hold a mind map of the entire code.

Box thinking and arrow thinking. Models can become great over time. The goal in complex system design is interoperability, not accuracy. We are in a “tathastu” era: whatever you imagine, you can build, fast. So people build something new quickly without taking time to design it to work with everything else. He called this box thinking, and asked for arrow thinking. You can improve your search engine, but who tells it to consult your inventory?

Privacy in a super-app world. His principle: keep behaviour separate from identity. In a hotel, the front desk sees your ID, but housekeeping only sees how you left the room, not who you are. He gave examples:

  • After 9/11, he was part of a team that built a US system to analyse flight bookings for suspicious patterns. The ACLU challenged it, and he said the ACLU won [check].
  • At Google, he built a system that mined emails to improve Gmail search auto-suggest [check]. It respected the identity rule, but legal review still blocked it, because even the perception of surfacing private conversations was too damaging.
  • In India, Aadhaar links to PAN, and PAN to income tax. Linking that to flight search could expose patterns about people.
  • A super-app that holds your insurance, food and health data must not reveal health data to the insurance side. School performance must not flow into college applications.
  • Enterprises now paste code and data into AI tools. The AI race is now about who has more customers and data, so many companies see that leak as a bigger problem than token cost.

Future jobs. The future jobs in AI won’t be data science. They will be AI architects and policy.
